# Writing hooks

A hook is the first line a reader sees. On LinkedIn it is the text before "...see more". On X it is the first line of the post. In email it is the subject plus the preview line. In video it is the first two seconds. Its only job is to get the next line read.

A good hook does one thing: it **makes one promise or opens one gap** that this reader wants closed. The rest of the post has to keep that promise.

## When this runs

Every time content is drafted, rewritten or reviewed, not only when someone asks for a hook. If the user asks for a whole post, write the body too, but still produce the 10 hooks, the checks table and a pick before showing the final draft. If they only want a quick edit to the middle of a piece, check the existing hook against the 5 checks in one line and move on.

## Before you write

Get these four things. If the user gave a draft post, read them from it. Ask only for what is missing, in one short question.

1. **Who reads it.** One person, not "everyone". "An artist manager running three artists" beats "music people".
2. **What the post gives them.** The single takeaway, result or story. If the post has no clear payoff, say so first: no hook can save a post with nothing in it.
3. **Where it goes.** Platform and format (see the limits below).
4. **House style.** Words or claims to avoid. If the project has a style file or a `references/` file for this brand, read it. Defaults below apply when nothing says otherwise.

## Why the first line gets this much work

People read the first few lines, then skim down the left edge catching only the first words of each line. On an average web page they read about 20 to 28% of the words. A hook gets even less time, so every word has to earn its place. Spend about half the writing time on the first line, not two minutes at the end.

## The method

1. **Write 10 hooks**, at least 5 different patterns from the list below. Volume is the point: the first idea is rarely the best.
2. **Run every hook through the 5 checks and the truth gate below.** Mark each check pass or fail. Keep only hooks that pass all 5 and the gate. If none do, rewrite the best ones until one does; don't lower the bar.
3. **Recommend one**, say why in one line, and give the runner-up.
4. **Guess test.** For the pick, write what you think the post says from the hook alone. Then tell the user to show it to 3 real people and ask them to guess (see check 2).
5. **Check the payoff.** Say in one line whether the post delivers what the chosen hook promises. If not, fix the hook (or flag the post), never the other way round with a bigger claim.

### The 5 checks

1. **Mirror the reader.** People stop for themselves, not for your topic. Put the reader's identity, or a specific moment from their life, inside the hook.
   - Weak: "Most people quit the gym, here's how to stay consistent."
   - Strong: "If you lied to yourself about hitting the gym again, you're teaching yourself that your word means nothing. Here's how to fix it:"
2. **Let them half guess the answer.** Curiosity peaks when the reader is about half sure what comes next. If they can't guess at all, they scroll. If they already know the ending, they scroll too. Test: show the hook to 3 people and ask what the post says. All 3 right means you gave away too much. None close means add one more piece of context.
3. **One surprise.** The eye goes to what the brain didn't predict, and on a feed the reader is predicting your line from the last 100 posts they saw on the topic. Add exactly one unexpected detail and keep everything else plain, so it stands out. Two surprises cancel each other.
4. **Exact numbers.** A precise number reads like someone counted. A round number reads like a guess. "11" beats "10", "37 tasks" beats "dozens". The number must still be true (see the gate).
5. **Readable in one breath.** Dead simple and short; nobody reads a hook twice. Read it aloud at normal speed: if you run out of breath before the end, cut until you don't. Then aim for reading grade 6 or lower (check in the free Hemingway Editor, hemingwayapp.com, and swap long words for short ones). When you can't run a tool, count: short words, one clause, no word over three syllables unless it is the reader's own term.

### The truth gate

Every number, name and claim is real, and the post delivers what the hook promises. Never invent a stat, a client or a result to pass check 4. If a pattern needs a number you don't have, use a different pattern or ask.

## Patterns

Each with a bare template. Fill with the user's real facts only.

1. **Specific result.** "We [did X] in [time]. Here's how." Needs a real number.
2. **Named pain.** Describe the moment the reader is stuck in, in their words. "Release is in 10 days and the cover art is still in someone's WhatsApp."
3. **Against the common belief.** "Most [people] think [belief]. [What actually happens]." Only if you can back it in the post.
4. **List with a promise.** "[N] things [reader] should [do] before [moment]." Exact numbers read as more honest than round ones.
5. **Mid-scene story.** Start inside the moment, no setup. "Two days before release, the distributor rejected the master."
6. **Mistake or lesson.** "I [did wrong thing] for [time]. It cost [real cost]."
7. **Direct question that stings.** A question the reader can't answer comfortably. "Who on your team knows the release date of your next single?" Avoid yes/no questions with an obvious answer.
8. **Before and after.** "[Old way]. Now [new way]." Works when the contrast is concrete.
9. **Call out the reader.** "If you manage more than one artist, read this." Use sparingly.
10. **Plain statement of something surprising.** A short true fact most readers don't know.

## Format limits

- **LinkedIn:** about 200 characters (2 to 3 short lines) show before "see more" on desktop, fewer on a phone. Put the whole hook in the first line, and keep line two worth reading. Line breaks matter.
- **X:** first line of the post, short. If it is a thread, the first post must stand alone.
- **Email subject:** under about 45 characters so it fits on a phone. The preview line is a second hook: don't waste it on "Hi".
- **DM opener:** about them, not you. One specific thing from their post or work, then the point. No pitch in line one.
- **Short video / reel:** what is said or shown in the first 2 seconds, plus the on-screen text.

## Default style (unless the project says otherwise)

- Plain words a 10-year-old understands. One idea per line.
- No em dashes. No hype words ("game-changer", "revolutionary", "unlock", "10x"). No startup cliches.
- No clickbait the post doesn't pay off. No fake urgency.
- No emoji at the start of a hook unless the user's voice uses them.
- Don't open with "I'm excited to announce", "In today's world", "Let's talk about", or a definition.

## Fixing an existing hook

When given a hook to fix: run it through the 5 checks and say which ones it fails, in one line. Then give 5 rewrites using different patterns, checked the same way, with a pick.

## Output format

Checks columns: M = mirror, G = half guess, S = one surprise, N = exact number, B = one breath. Use ✓ or ✗. N can be "-" when the hook has no number and doesn't need one.

```
Reader: <one line>
Payoff: <one line>

| # | Hook | Pattern | M | G | S | N | B | True |
|---|------|---------|---|---|---|---|---|------|
| 1 | ...  | ...     | ✓ | ✓ | ✗ | - | ✓ | ✓    |

Pick: #N, because <one line>.
Runner-up: #M.
My guess from the hook alone: <what the post says>. Show it to 3 people before posting.
Payoff check: <does the post deliver it?>
```

Keep it to that. Don't lecture on hook theory unless asked.

## Source

The 5 checks, the guess test, the one-breath test and the "half your time on the hook" habit come from George's X article "Learn how to write scientific hooks" (@georgewritess, Nov 2026). The research he cites (eye tracking, own-name attention, curiosity peaking near 50% confidence, a 2006 study on sharp versus round numbers) is his summary and has not been checked by us.

## Brand files

Put brand-specific context in `references/<brand>.md` (who reads, words to avoid, real numbers that can be used). This repo ships `references/urganize.md`. When working for that brand, read it first.
